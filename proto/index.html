Planet scale — three lenses on one world

Same world, same paint, same drawing pass. The only thing that changes between the three is the lens: how a point's longitude and altitude become a place on the screen. Drag any panel to travel round the rim — all three stay locked to the same spot.

Prototype 1

Rim — exaggerate the altitude

One true circle. Longitude is honest all the way round; altitude is not. The forty metres above sea level get as much screen as the five hundred kilometres below it, so mountains, forests, ice and coastline all read at a glance while the whole planet is in frame.

Towns below three pixels wide are drawn as marks, not geometry — a world has to look inhabited from orbit.

Turn relief to zero to see what the skew buys: a true-scale planet is a plain circle with a hairline of sky. Turn zoom up to see the honest cost of having no lens at all — detail only arrives once the planet has left the frame.

Prototype 2

Fisheye — redistribute the longitude

The disc never changes size and never leaves the screen. A window of ground around the focus is spread across a fixed arc of about 110°, and the entire rest of the planet is compressed logarithmically into what is left — so every decade of distance from where you are looking costs the same slice of screen.

The falloff is solved, not tuned: it is the value that makes the angular scale continuous across the edge of the window. Guess and there is a visible kink in every coastline crossing it.

This is the one that answers the brief literally: at full zoom the dashed window holds a few hundred metres of street, the far side of the world is still on screen, and it is one continuous circle with no cut in it.

Prototype 3

Ladder — repeat the circle

The planet stays a small undistorted disc. Each ring outside it is one slice of the ring within, unrolled and magnified by the same factor — so the marked wedge is always one part in step of whatever it sits on, and the plumb line says all four are looking at the same place.

Ground sits on each ring's inner edge with the sky filling outward, so up is outward everywhere, planet included.

Every scale is on screen at once and nothing is distorted — but each rung is a thin band, and you read it rather than fly through it. This is the instrument, not the window.
